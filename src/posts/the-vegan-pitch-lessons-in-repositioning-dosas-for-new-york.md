<p>In most parts of India, dosas are ubiquitous. They&rsquo;re a breakfast fare and comfort food. Their place in Indian culture is already firmly established. Most Indian restaurants don&rsquo;t bother advertising dosas separately &ndash; they&rsquo;re simply expected to be on the menu, much like coffee shops don&rsquo;t specially promote that they serve coffee.</p>

<p>The exceptions are those <a href="https://www.youtube.com/shorts/gSnFrk7j_po">Instagram-ready QSRs</a> where dosas swim in pools of ghee, designed more for viral content than authentic experience. These represent marketing at work, not tradition.</p>

<p>But travel 8,000 miles to New York City, and this same dish transforms into something entirely different &mdash; not through a change in recipe, but through an interesting shift in positioning.</p>

<h3>&ldquo;The first vegan dosa cart in the world&rdquo;: Meet NYC&rsquo;s Dosa Man</h3>

<p>Step into Greenwich Village, however, and you&rsquo;ll find NY Dosas &mdash; a humble food cart with an extraordinary reputation. Here, Thiru Kumar (aka the &ldquo;Dosa Man&rdquo;) has been serving dosas since 2001, winning the Vendy Award (the Oscars of street food) and attracting long lines of devoted customers.</p>

<p>I&rsquo;ve been following his journey for years, watching how this unassuming cart transformed into a culinary destination that food enthusiasts from around the world seek out when visiting New York.</p>

<p>His Instagram and X accounts are filled with photos of happy customers taking selfies with their dosas and the charismatic Dosa Man himself.</p>

<figure><img src="/wp-content/uploads/linkedin/dosa-man.png" alt=""></figure>

<p>What makes Kumar&rsquo;s approach fascinating is his positioning. &ldquo;This is the first vegan dosa cart in the world,&rdquo; Kumar proudly states about his cart. Where most food vendors in India would simply call themselves a &ldquo;dosa stall,&rdquo; Kumar specifically markets his business as a &ldquo;vegetarian vegan dosa cart in Washington Square Park.&rdquo;</p>

<p>In a city dominated by fast food, Kumar recognized an opportunity to differentiate through conscious positioning: &ldquo;New York is known for hot dogs and cheese kebab and stuff no one expects from the tiny cart like this. We&rsquo;re providing good, healthy food.&rdquo;</p>

<p>Kumar doesn&rsquo;t just sell dosas &mdash; he sells a narrative about health and conscious eating. When describing his food, he emphasizes attributes that would sometimes go unmentioned in India: &ldquo;Dosa is a plate made of rice and lentil is naturally fermented totally gluten-free.&rdquo; The words &ldquo;naturally fermented&rdquo; and &ldquo;gluten-free&rdquo; are marketing gold in health-conscious New York, despite being inherent qualities that Indian vendors rarely highlight.</p>

<h3>How context transforms messaging</h3>

<figure><img src="/wp-content/uploads/linkedin/dosa-eater.png" alt="Thiru Kumar at his NY Dosas cart"><figcaption>Source: Eater</figcaption></figure>

<p>Audience context determines which product attributes to emphasize. The same dish can be positioned entirely differently depending on who you&rsquo;re selling to and what they value.</p>

<p>Kumar understood this intuitively: &ldquo;New York is a very busy city, everyone&rsquo;s running. No time for nothing, so they&rsquo;re craving good, healthy food, you know. That&rsquo;s why I started to do this.&rdquo;</p>

<p>What&rsquo;s remarkable is that Kumar didn&rsquo;t change the dosa recipe to make it vegan &mdash; it already was. In India, a dosa&rsquo;s plant-based nature isn&rsquo;t portrayed as a feature &mdash; it&rsquo;s just how dosas have always been made. But in New York, where veganism is a conscious lifestyle choice rather than a cultural default, Kumar cleverly repositions this inherent quality as a key selling point.</p>

<p>He takes pride in how his messaging resonates with this audience: his dosas &ldquo;give you natural energy, that&rsquo;s what all the vegans are very happy, you know.&rdquo; By emphasizing the vegan angle, Kumar changes what would be an unremarkable trait in India into a compelling value proposition for New Yorkers.</p>

<h3>Sacred recipes and cultural translation</h3>

<p>Thiru&rsquo;s dosa evolution, based on his grandmother&rsquo;s recipe, from everyday Indian staple to celebrated vegan specialty, carries valuable lessons:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Position based on audience values, not tradition:</strong> Kumar didn&rsquo;t change what dosas are, but changed how he talks about them based on what New Yorkers value.</li>
<li><strong>Highlight inherent attributes that resonate locally:</strong> Features like &ldquo;naturally fermented&rdquo; and &ldquo;gluten-free&rdquo; were always part of dosas, but are emphasized differently in New York.</li>
<li><strong>See your product through fresh eyes:</strong> Sometimes what seems ordinary in one context becomes your strongest selling point in another.</li>
<li><strong>Authenticity + right positioning = success:</strong> NY Dosas succeeds because it maintains the authentic essence of dosa while consciously framing it for a new audience.</li>
</ul>

<p>The next time I encounter something, I consider how the same thing can tell entirely different stories depending on who&rsquo;s listening. It&rsquo;s a delicious reminder that effective brand marketing often isn&rsquo;t about changing your product, but rather about changing how you position it within a new cultural context.</p>
